## 2. Origin story

Rainforest, Inc. was founded in March 2017 in Columbus, Ohio, by **Evelyn Marsh** and **Tomás
Rivera**. Marsh, who served as CEO from 2017 to 2025, came to the company as an ex-VP Logistics
at a national grocery chain; she grew up over her family's Dayton hardware store, and the
company's operating culture — count everything, promise only what the dock can ship — traces
directly to that upbringing. Rivera, the company's CTO, was previously a principal engineer at a
freight-routing startup. The two met on a stalled I-70 freight corridor project, where a shared
frustration with how slowly regional freight actually moved became the founding thesis: the
infrastructure for next-morning regional commerce did not exist, so they would build it.

The company started as next-morning delivery of household essentials within the Columbus metro,
operating from one leased warehouse — **"Warehouse Zero" on Alum Creek Drive**. The premise was
deliberately narrow: a constrained catalog, a single metro, and one uncompromising promise —
ordered by evening, on the doorstep by tomorrow morning. Warehouse Zero's discipline (dense
racking, obsessive slotting, a delivery cutoff the whole building organized around) became the
operational template that every subsequent facility inherited.

From that single building, Rainforest expanded to a **14-state Midwest/Mid-Atlantic footprint**,
adding fulfillment centers along the interstate corridors its founders knew intimately. By 2025
the network comprised 11 fulfillment centers serving a region that national players treat as
flyover freight lanes — and that Rainforest treats as home.

## 3. Mission & values

Our mission: **"Everything your region needs, by tomorrow morning."**

Four values govern how Rainforest operates. They are printed on the wall of every fulfillment
center, beginning with Warehouse Zero.

**Velocity.** Speed is the product. A regional network wins on the hours national networks
cannot recover — the evening cutoff, the overnight linehaul, the morning doorstep. Every process
at Rainforest is designed backward from the delivery promise, and every hour added anywhere in
the chain must justify itself against it.

**Thrift.** Rainforest was built in Ohio, not on a coastal balance sheet. We spend where the
customer feels it and nowhere else. Thrift is not austerity — it is the discipline that keeps a
regional operator alive between giants, and it is the value the "One Basket" years tested most
severely.

**Candor.** Bad numbers surface fast, or they compound. We report operational reality — on-time
rates, ticket volumes, margin erosion — without varnish, internally and to shareholders. The
operational challenges documented in §9 of this report are candor in practice: they are stated
plainly because that is how they get fixed.

**Customer gravity.** Every decision orbits the customer's actual experience, not our internal
convenience. When the data says customers are waiting on late inbound freight, the answer is not
a better excuse — it is a different supplier. Customer gravity is why the company measures itself
in tickets per thousand orders and not in press releases.

## 4. Growth and IPO (2017–2023)

The growth years validated the regional thesis. Revenue grew from **$310.7M in FY2021 to
$650.0M in FY2023** — a **44.6% revenue CAGR** — on GMV that rose from $780.0M (FY2021) to
$1,198.3M (FY2023). Through this period the business was still majority third-party marketplace:
first-party share of GMV was 30.0% in 2021-Q1 and 33.0% at the end of 2021, with the marketplace
take rate at 12.0%. Order volume grew from 2.91M orders in 2021-Q1 to 4.83M in 2023-Q4, while
active customers grew from 1.18M to 2.64M over the same span. Net income was positive in every
quarter of 2021–2023 (+$10.1M FY2021, +$7.3M FY2022, +$8.9M FY2023) — modest, deliberate
profitability in the Thrift tradition.

In **May 2023** the company completed its initial public offering on **NASDAQ: RAIN, priced at
$21/share**. The offering funded network expansion, and the post-IPO hiring wave landed
immediately: headcount jumped from 2,760 (2023-Q2) to 3,310 (2023-Q4) — **+19.9% in the two
quarters after the offering**.

Operationally, the network entered the public markets in strong form: on-time delivery stood at
96.0% in 2023-Q1 and support tickets ran at 9.1 per 1,000 orders. Those two figures matter,
because the following two years would test both.

## 5. "One Basket" and the stall (2022–2025)

In Q1-2022 the company announced **"One Basket"**: aggressive first-party expansion, the
**`Rainforest Basics`** private label, and universal free same-day delivery. The strategic logic
was coherent at the time. Owning more of the basket meant owning more of the margin and the
customer relationship; a private label meant capturing brand economics; free same-day delivery
meant a moat no regional competitor could match. First-party sales also book gross revenue, and
the top line responded exactly as designed — revenue more than doubled from $467.3M (FY2022) to
$1,101.8M (FY2024).

The numbers underneath told a different story, and the bible records the turn quarter by quarter:

- **First-party share of GMV rose from 34% (2022-Q1) to 61% (2024-Q4)** — a monotone climb, the
  strategy executing precisely as announced.
- **Gross margin fell from 24% (2022-Q4) to 14% (2025-Q2)** — the mirror image of the 1P climb,
  as gross-booked, low-margin first-party sales and free same-day economics diluted the P&L.
- Fulfillment cost per order rose from $5.10 (2022-Q1) to $7.60 (2025-Q2) as free same-day zones
  expanded ahead of the density needed to pay for them.
- Net income went negative in 2024-Q2 (−$6.5M) and worsened each quarter to −$21.6M in 2025-Q2 —
  **five straight loss-making quarters by mid-2025**.
- Revenue plateaued: 2025-Q1 through 2025-Q3 printed $331.1M, $333.1M, $331.7M — each quarter
  within ±2% of the prior. The growth story had stalled at almost exactly the moment the losses
  peaked.

The operational base decayed in parallel. On-time delivery declined from **96.0% (2023-Q1) to
88.0% (2025-Q3)**, and support tickets per 1,000 orders climbed from **9 to 19** over the same
window, lagging the on-time decline by one quarter. The causes were identifiable and are treated
honestly in §9: two chronically late suppliers concentrated in the very categories One Basket had
expanded into, and an unhedged tariff exposure that pushed electronics landed costs up 18%.

One Basket was not an irrational strategy. It was a national player's strategy executed on a
regional operator's balance sheet, and by mid-2025 the P&L had rendered its verdict.

## 6. Leadership transition (August 2025)

In **August 2025** the Board hired **Priya Raghavan** as Chief Executive Officer. Raghavan joined
as ex-COO of Meridian Retail Group, where she ran large-format retail operations at several times
Rainforest's scale. **Evelyn Marsh moved to Executive Chair**, remaining the company's public
voice on culture and its long-term steward, with Raghavan owning operations and strategy.

The Board's rationale was straightforward and consistent with the Candor value: five consecutive
loss-making quarters, a stalled top line, and an operational decay curve (on-time delivery,
ticket rates) that the existing strategy had no mechanism to reverse. The company did not need a
new mission — the next-morning promise was intact and differentiated. It needed an operator who
had run fulfillment infrastructure as a business rather than as a cost center, and who could
subordinate the 1P catalog to the economics rather than the other way around.

The transition was deliberately sequenced. Raghavan's first quarter (2025-Q3) produced no numeric
effect — the bible shows the inflection beginning only in 2025-Q4, after the new strategy was
announced. What she spent that first quarter doing is visible in §7.

## 7. The Fulfillment Flywheel (Q4-2025 →)

In Q4-2025 Raghavan announced **"The Fulfillment Flywheel"**: open Rainforest's warehouses to
third-party merchants as the **Rainforest Fulfillment Network (RFN)**; shrink the first-party
catalog to data-proven winners; exit unprofitable same-day zones; and run operations with AI
agents. This application — the operations control plane in this repository — is that last plank
made concrete: agent-run fulfillment, inventory, and reordering with human oversight and a full
audit trail.

The Flywheel inverts One Basket's logic. Rainforest's differentiated asset was never the basket —
it was the network: 11 fulfillment centers and a next-morning promise across 14 states. RFN turns
that asset into the product, converting fixed fulfillment capacity into take-rate revenue that
carries no inventory risk. Catalog rationalization keeps only the first-party SKUs the data
proves out (including a culled `Rainforest Basics` line), and the agent-run operating model
attacks the cost base that free same-day delivery had inflated.

The early green shoots are in the bible, beginning exactly at the pivot and not before:

- **Gross margin turned at 2025-Q4** (14.2% → 15.0%) and recovered to **17.5% by 2026-Q3**.
- **First-party share peaked at 64.0% in 2025-Q3 and fell to 52% by 2026-Q3** — the catalog
  rationalization executing.
- **Net income turned positive in 2026-Q2** (+$4.2M, then +$9.8M in 2026-Q3), after the loss had
  already narrowed from −$16.2M (2025-Q3) to −$9.4M (2025-Q4) and −$3.1M (2026-Q1).
- **Marketplace (3P) GMV grew +9.7% QoQ in 2026-Q1 and +10.1% QoQ in 2026-Q2** — RFN adoption
  showing up in the mix.
- **On-time delivery recovered to 92.0% by 2026-Q2** (from the 88.0% trough), driven by the
  supplier actions described in §9.1, and fulfillment cost per order fell from $7.20 (2025-Q4)
  to $6.10 (2026-Q3).

One line deserves candor: total revenue drifts down through 2026 ($346.6M in 2025-Q4 to $318.5M
in 2026-Q3) even as GMV grows. That is the arithmetic of health, not decline — the mix is
shifting from gross-booked 1P sales to take-rate commission on 3P GMV. A dollar of RFN revenue is
worth structurally more than a dollar of One Basket revenue, and the margin and net-income lines
say so.

## 9. Operational challenges

Candor requires this section. The following three assessments are written as they were presented
internally — they are the operational seams the AI-agent control plane in this repository is
built to surface and act on, and every figure traces to the bible and the data specs.

### 9.1 Chronically late suppliers

Two suppliers, concentrated in the categories One Basket expanded hardest into, drove the on-time
decay. **Brightline Electronics Co.** (Shenzhen) ran at **71% on-time, mean 8.5 days late,
worsening through 2024–2025**. **Apex Plastics Manufacturing** (Dongguan) ran at **76%
on-time**. As a control that isolates the problem to these vendors rather than to our network,
**Great Lakes Packaging** (Toledo, OH) held **98%** on-time throughout.

The internal assessment is unflattering: procurement flagged Brightline's slippage in 2024, but
under One Basket's volume commitments there was no mechanism to route around a strategic 1P
supplier, and the lateness flowed straight through to the customer promise — on-time delivery
fell from 96% (2023-Q1) to 88% (2025-Q3) with these two vendors' inbound delays as the causal
driver. Post-pivot, the remediation was structural, not exhortative: Brightline was dropped, and
**Saigon Circuit Works** (Ho Chi Minh City) was **onboarded in Q1-2026 at 95% on-time**. On-time
delivery recovered to 92% by 2026-Q2. The auto-reorder agent now penalizes SLA laggards in
supplier selection by policy, so this failure mode cannot silently recur.

### 9.2 Tariff exposure in electronics and small kitchen appliances

Import tariffs pushed the landed-cost index for consumer electronics and small kitchen
appliances from **100 (Q1-2024) to 118 (Q4-2025)** — **+18%** in eight quarters — and category
gross margin absorbed **−6 points**. The honest assessment: the exposure was **unhedged and
un-mitigated until the pivot**. One Basket had deepened first-party ownership of exactly the
categories with the worst tariff trajectory, so the company was long the risk with no sourcing
diversification, no pricing pass-through discipline, and no hedge. The surfaced recommendation,
now executing under the Flywheel: **shift electronics sourcing to Vietnam/Mexico suppliers** —
the Saigon Circuit Works onboarding (§9.1) is the first concrete step, and the landed-cost index
has eased from its 118.0 peak (2025-Q4) to 114.8 (2026-Q3).

### 9.3 Support-ticket clusters corroborate the operational data

The support queue independently confirms both stories above. The **shipping-delay complaint
cluster tracks the on-time-% decline with a one-quarter lag and concentrates on Brightline/
Apex-sourced SKUs** — customers complained about precisely the SKUs those two suppliers fed,
one quarter after each on-time dip. A second cluster of **`Rainforest Basics` quality complaints
peaks in 2024–2025**, corroborating that the private label scaled faster than its quality
controls. **Billing tickets stayed flat throughout** — the control cluster proving the queue's
growth was operational, not noise. In aggregate, tickets per 1,000 orders rose from 9 to 19
(2023 → 2025-Q4) and have since fallen back to 14.2 (2026-Q3). The three-way corroboration —
supplier SLA data, on-time trend, ticket clusters — is exactly the analytical walk this
application's insight surfaces are designed to let an operator make in minutes rather than
quarters.

## 10. About this repository

**Rainforest, Inc. is a fiction.** Every entity in this document — the company, its founders and
executives, its suppliers (Brightline Electronics Co., Apex Plastics Manufacturing, Great Lakes
Packaging, Saigon Circuit Works), Meridian Retail Group, the NASDAQ ticker RAIN, and every
financial figure — is invented. None of it refers to any real company, person, or security. Any
resemblance to real entities is coincidental. All data in this repository is synthetic, generated
to match the canonical dataset in `data/numbers-bible.json`.

This repository is a proving ground: it specifies an enterprise inventory, order, and
supply-chain demo application that is **built autonomously by an AI agent** as a demonstration of
autonomous software delivery. The business fiction above exists to give that demonstration
realistic operational texture — including deliberately planted data stories (§9) for AI-driven
insight surfacing to "discover." Nothing in this document is investment advice, a real financial
statement, or a description of a real business.
